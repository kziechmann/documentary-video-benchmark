PASADENA, Calif. — Scientists of the Jet Propulsion Laboratory and NASA today released the complete set of television pictures taken by the Mariner 4 spacecraft during its flyby of Mars on July 14 and 15.

The spacecraft, launched from Cape Kennedy on November 28, 1964, passed within approximately 6,100 miles of the planet. Its television camera took a sequence of pictures over about 25 minutes, recording them on magnetic tape for later transmission to Earth.

Twenty-one complete pictures were received, together with a portion of a twenty-second frame. Because of the great distance — more than 130 million miles — the pictures were transmitted at a rate of 8⅓ bits per second, so that each picture took more than eight hours to reach the tracking stations of the Deep Space Network, including the Goldstone station in California's Mojave Desert.

The pictures show that the surface of Mars is marked by craters resembling those of the Moon. More than 70 craters were identified in the frames, ranging up to about 120 kilometers across. The scientific team reported no indication of the "canals" long reported by some observers using Earth-based telescopes.

Measurements made as the spacecraft's radio signal passed through the Martian atmosphere indicate that the pressure at the surface is only a few thousandths of that at the surface of the Earth — far thinner than had been generally assumed.

Mariner 4 was the second of two spacecraft built for the 1964 Mars opportunity. Its twin, Mariner 3, was launched on November 5, 1964, but failed when the protective shroud covering the spacecraft did not separate after launch.
